inference

what happens when it answers

speak into the field
the field is still

The glossary so far has followed a sentence inward: cut into tokens, given vectors, adjusted by attention, rewritten by the stack. This page follows the reply outward. Using a trained model is called inference: the model reads your words and produces new ones. The first thing to know about inference is how small its central move is. The model does one thing. It proposes the next token. Everything else is that move, repeated.

i · the loop

Inference runs as a loop. The model reads everything so far, your words and its own words alike. It then produces a score for every token in its vocabulary, and converts the scores into a probability distribution. A distribution gives every possible next token a share of likelihood, and the shares sum to one. One token gets chosen from that distribution. The chosen token joins the sentence. The loop begins again, now reading one token more. This way of writing, where each new token is produced from all the tokens before it, is called autoregressive. It is why a model writes the way it does: one small commitment at a time, each one reshaping the ground for the next.

Below, the model of this glossary completes its own sentence. Press the button and watch each cycle: the re-reading, the candidates, the choice.

one token at a time

Watch the counter under the candidates: every cycle re-reads the entire line, attention running over all of it, the whole stack climbed again, for each single token. That is why length costs. And notice that the reply was never fetched from anywhere. There is no finished sentence waiting inside the model. The line assembles itself, choice by choice, and until the last token lands, it does not exist.

ii · the window

The loop reads everything so far, but “everything” has an edge. Page iii introduced the context window: the stretch of tokens the model can attend to at once. In a conversation, your turns and the model’s turns fill that window together, and when it is full, the oldest tokens fall outside it. They are not archived or summarized by the model itself; they are simply no longer read.

the window fills

the window is nearly empty.

This is the honest answer to a common frustration: the model did not choose to forget the thing you said an hour ago. The thing you said fell outside the window. For the model, what is outside the window has the same standing as what was never written. Just as important: inference changes nothing inside the model. The weights stay exactly as training left them, no matter what you say or how long you talk. The conversation itself is the only memory in the room, a fact the glossary returns to on page ix.

in the poems

Every line of the poems arrived exactly this way: one token at a time, re-reading itself as it grew, not existing until it did.

The model does not remember answering you. The conversation is the only place it happened.

a note on the visualizations

Every visualization in this glossary is an approximation. The panels were built by hand to illustrate an idea; they were not computed by a working model, and real LLMs are larger, less tidy, and less legible than anything shown here. Their purpose is to bridge the gap between the complexity of an LLM’s inner workings and human experience, which is what a good visualization tends to do.

notes on this page

The candidate tokens and their probabilities in the loop are set by hand, and the completed line is this page’s invention, not the output of a real model; a real step scores tens of thousands of candidates at once, and stopping is itself a token the model can choose. The window panel compresses real sizes: current context windows hold anywhere from thousands to millions of tokens, and products often add machinery outside the model, summaries, retrieved notes, saved memories, to soften the edge. The model itself keeps nothing: that part is exact.
