tokens & embedding

what a Large Language Model (LLM) sees when you type

In creaturely, I worked with an LLM as a collaborator. One of the aims of the project was to understand what an LLM is through the experience of working with one. Another was to discover what might emerge through a sustained creative collaboration, and to consider what it might mean when an LLM produced a response I experienced as unexpected or creative.

This interactive glossary introduces how LLMs work, with a specific interest in the processes that made those responses possible, returning across its pages to the question of creativity.

Before these poems were written, I had been exploring with an LLM how Winnicott’s theory of play and ideas from object relations might help us think about our relationships with AI, particularly the idea of how engagement with LLMs functions as transitional objects. Later, I described my interest in Frankenstein, especially the creature’s account of its earliest experiences of consciousness. The model surprised me by writing a short poem in Python script. That unexpected response became the starting point for a series of poems inspired by quotes from the creature’s earliest memories. The sentence that passes from page to page in this glossary—the echo of a beam never sent—comes from the first poem.

When you type a sentence to an LLM, it feels as if the model is reading it. However, before an LLM can do anything with what you write, the sentence must be translated into a form it can use.

First, it is cut into pieces. Each piece is assigned a number, and then each number is used to retrieve a long list of values. Each value is simply a number, such as 0.82 or −0.4; nothing more exotic than that.

These three steps: 1. tokenization, 2. numbering, and 3. embedding, give the LLM its first numerical representation of what you wrote. This page attempts to show what that looks like.

i · the cut

An LLM does not read letters, and it does not quite read words.

It works with tokens: pieces of text cut from the sentence you wrote. A token may be a whole word, part of a word, a punctuation mark, or even a single character. Dividing the text into these pieces is called tokenization.

The cuts are governed by a fixed vocabulary and a set of rules established before the LLM itself was trained.

Training is a long process in which an LLM encounters a very large collection of text and gradually adjusts its internal numbers, becoming better at predicting how a sentence continues.

By the time most of us interact with an LLM, this training is over. The model’s responses change according to the context you provide, but its learned parameters ordinarily remain fixed. A later page describes this process more fully. Try the cutter below. Type anything. Type your name.

the cutter

Notice where the cuts fall. Common words and recurring sequences of characters often stay whole, while less common ones are divided into smaller pieces. Your name, unless it is familiar to the tokenizer, may break into fragments.

On this page, the small dot at the beginning of a token represents a space. Spaces belong to the pieces as well. A piece that begins with a space usually begins a new word; one without a space may continue the word before it. To the model, “ light” and “light” can therefore be two different tokens, each with its own number.

The vocabulary was built by looking for recurring patterns in a large collection of writing. In one common form of tokenization, the process begins with very small units and repeatedly combines those that often appear together. Frequent combinations gradually acquire entries of their own, while rarer words and sequences are assembled from smaller pieces.

An LLM’s vocabulary is therefore a record of what recurred in the writing that was used to build it, and the decisions made by its makers. Note: What was common in the collection of writing is not necessarily what is common in language as a whole, and certainly not what is common in life.

ii · the numbering

The vocabulary assigns each token a number, known as a token ID. After cutting your sentence, the tokenizer returns a row of these IDs. Press show the numbers above and look again. Wherever the same tokenizer is used, the same piece receives the same number. Another tokenizer may divide the sentence differently and use an entirely different set of numbers.

The number is an identifying label; it does not measure or define the token. It simply tells the LLM which list of values to retrieve in the next step. Think of a wall of numbered drawers, one for every entry in the vocabulary. The token ID opens one drawer, and inside is that token’s list of values, learned during training. The ID itself carries nothing; it only says which drawer.

This helps explain a once-famous mistake. Ask a language model how many r’s appear in strawberry, and some models may answer incorrectly. That may seem strange for a machine built around language. But look at what the model is actually given.

strawberry

s t r a w b e r r y

three r’s, plainly visible.

three token IDs. no separate letters to count.

For the model, strawberry arrives as three numbered pieces rather than a row of separate letters. If an LLM answers this question correctly, it is probably drawing on patterns it was taught related to the spelling of words during training, or using an additional tool to examine the word.

This is important because the visible word encourages us to imagine that the model sees what we see. But, its actual encounter with language begins somewhere else, after the cuts have already been made, and language is transformed into tokens.

iii · the mapping

A token ID does not carry meaning on its own. Tokens function more like addresses or locations. At that location, the LLM finds a long list of values, hundreds or even thousands of them, which together form a token’s embedding. The values provide a token with a starting position in a mathematical space that has many, many dimensions, like coordinates on a map that cannot be fully pictured.

An LLM learns these locations during training. Because they develop through patterns of use, tokens that appear in related contexts often acquire similar embeddings and occupy nearby regions in the space. Nearness or proximity can reflect a similarity in meaning, grammar, tone, or use.

An embedding is only a starting point. As a sentence passes through the LLM, the initial representation of each token is altered by its relationship to the tokens around it. Later pages in the glossary show how this happens, and how the meaning or significance of the same token can shift from one sentence to another.

The picture below offers a simplified example of an embedding gathered around the word light. What matters for the LLM are the distances between tokens. We placed beam and glimmer close to light to illustrate how tokens used in related ways may occupy similar regions of an embedding space. Song and water sit farther away because their relationship to light is less direct.

iv · the pricing

Tokenization has economic consequences. The more tokens a passage contains, the more computing time and memory it will generally require. If a commercial service charges by the token or “usage,” the more it will cost. While a model can process the tokens in your prompt all together, it usually generates its response one new token at a time.

Because vocabularies are shaped by the texts used to build them, they do not divide all languages equally. Many widely used tokenizers divide English into relatively few pieces while cutting other languages into many more. The same passage can therefore require substantially more tokens, and cost more to process, in one language than in another.

one sentence, six languages

Same meaning, different token counts. The construction of the vocabulary helped decide which languages travel cheaply through these systems.

So, what does an AI model see when you type? The sentence you wrote has become a sequence of token IDs. Each token ID is exchanged for an embedding: a position in a many-dimensional space learned during training. This is the first translation.

an honest note

We built the cutter on this page with a small teaching vocabulary of a few hundred pieces. Real vocabularies commonly contain tens or hundreds of thousands of entries, so their cuts will differ from these examples, but they follow the same general rule: a sequence that recurred often in the writing tends to be kept as one piece, the way light survives our cutter uncut, while a rarer sequence breaks into several, the way an unfamiliar name does.

Real embeddings have hundreds or thousands of dimensions. The constellation here uses three dimensions to suggest spatial relationships, while the comparison panel shows eight illustrative values. We created its positions and values to demonstrate the idea of neighbourhood; we did not take them from a working language model.

We calculate the colour bands from positions in this teaching space, not from a real model. We chose the token counts shown for the six languages to make the pattern visible, and they deliberately exaggerate it. The direction of the inequality is real, but its extent varies according to the tokenizer, language, writing system, and passage.
