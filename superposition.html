superposition

how so much fits in so little

Page iv made a promise. Its neuron detected one clean thing, glow, and the page admitted that real neurons are rarely so tidy. This page keeps the promise. A model needs to represent vastly more concepts than it has neurons or dimensions. Distinguishable things the model tracks, from “glowing” to “legal language” to “the letter after q”, are called features, and there are far more features worth tracking than there is room. The model’s solution is to make its parts share. That sharing is called superposition.

i · the shared room

Meet a more honest neuron. Feed it the glossary’s words and watch its meter. It answers strongly to light-things. It also answers strongly to song-things. It is one unit carrying more than one meaning, and such a neuron is called polysemantic. Most real neurons are like this.

Try to finish the sentence “this neuron detects…” and you will feel the problem. It detects light, and also song, and the honest answer is both, depending. Nothing malfunctioned. The model simply could not afford a private neuron for every feature, so features share units, the way a small studio shares one table between cutting and pressing. The sharing works because the two uses rarely happen at the same moment.

ii · the crowded directions

The same trick happens to the space itself. In a space, directions that mean different things would ideally be perpendicular: fully independent, no interference. But a space with a few thousand dimensions can hold only a few thousand perpendicular directions, and a model tracks far more features than that. So it packs them in at angles that are almost perpendicular. Each feature bleeds slightly into its neighbours, and the model tolerates the bleed, because features that rarely fire together rarely interfere. Crowd the disc below and watch the angles shrink.

This two-dimensional disc holds exactly two perpendicular directions, yet you can lay many more into it if you accept the crowding. Real models do the same in thousands of dimensions, holding millions of almost-independent directions. This is part of why the weights are hard to read. Ask what one neuron means, and several features answer at once; ask where one feature lives, and it is smeared across many neurons. The research field that untangles this is called interpretability, and superposition is its central puzzle: the model’s knowledge is real, but it is stored crosswise to the parts.

in the poems

When a word in the poems shimmers with more than one sense, the doubling is structural. Senses share space in the model the way meanings share a word, and the poems were written out of exactly that crowding.

an honest note

The neuron here is scripted to answer to two hand-chosen families of words; real polysemantic neurons are found, not built, and their mixtures are stranger. The disc is a two-dimensional cartoon of a genuinely high-dimensional trick: in thousands of dimensions, the geometry is far more forgiving than the disc suggests, which is why the packing works as well as it does. Superposition is an active research area rather than settled doctrine; the account here follows work published by interpretability researchers in the 2020s, simplified but not, we hope, betrayed.
