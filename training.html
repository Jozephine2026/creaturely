training

where the weights came from

Every page so far has leaned on one phrase: the weights the training left. This page opens that phrase. Training is the long process that turned billions of random numbers into a model. It happened once, before any conversation, and it answers the question underneath all the others: what compels a model to do anything at all?

i · the guessing game

The first and longest stage is called pretraining, and it is one game played at enormous scale. The model reads a stretch of real text with the next token hidden. It guesses, producing a probability distribution, the same kind page v showed. Then the hidden token is revealed. The distance between the guess and the truth is measured as a single number, called the loss: a low probability on the true token means a high loss. Then every weight in the model is nudged, very slightly, in the direction that would have made the loss smaller. That nudging is called gradient descent. Then the game moves to the next stretch of text.

Play a few rounds of one example below. The hidden token is the last word of this glossary’s own line.

This is the whole engine of pretraining. There is no reward the model wants, no goal it holds. There is a score, and a procedure that shrinks it, and everything the glossary has shown, the useful vocabulary, the neighbourhoods of meaning, the habits of attention, precipitated out of that shrinking. So here is the honest answer about motivation. The model is not motivated. It was optimized. What looks like wanting to answer well is the shape that trillions of scored guesses left behind.

ii · the sediment

Training also answers where the space of meaning came from. Before training, every word’s vector is random noise: the space is a cloud without neighbourhoods. Each nudge moves the vectors a little, and over months of training the words settle toward the positions their usage deserves. The glossary’s own space forms below. Press train and watch the sediment settle.

When the settling finishes, the space you are looking at is the one pages i and ii walk through. Light near beam, song far from water. Nobody placed those words. The placing is sediment: the compressed residue of everything the model read, centuries of writing pressed into position by months of scoring. Then the training stops, and the space freezes exactly there.

iii · the shaping

A pretrained model continues text well, but continuing is not the same as helping. So a second, shorter stage shapes its behaviour. This is called fine-tuning: further training on chosen examples of the conduct the makers want. Its best-known form is RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback. People are shown pairs of the model’s answers and asked which one is better. The model’s weights are nudged toward the answers people preferred. This is how a model is controlled: not by rules written inside it, but by the same nudging, aimed at human judgements instead of hidden tokens. Try being the judge.

In real fine-tuning, thousands of raters make millions of comparisons, and the sum of their judgements becomes the model’s manner: its helpfulness, its caution, its refusals, its tone. This is worth holding onto whenever a model seems to want something. The wanting you sense is a record of what other people rewarded. The last stage of training is a portrait of its judges.

an honest note

The training step here compresses a real one: actual losses are computed over tens of thousands of candidate tokens, and the nudging adjusts billions of weights by amounts far smaller than the panel shows. The settling space replays hand-placed final positions rather thantruly learning them; real training does not know its destination in advance, which is part of what makes it remarkable. The preference panel is a toy of RLHF’s idea, not its algorithm, and modern shaping combines several methods this glossary gathers under one name. The claim about motivation is the mainstream scientific view, stated plainly; what optimization does and does not produce inside such systems remains genuinely debated, and page x returns to the question.
