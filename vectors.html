creaturely: a glossary · ii

vectors

how meaning becomes distance and direction

The first page of this glossary shows how a sentence is cut into numbered pieces, and how an LLM places each piece at a position in a space with many dimensions. This page introduces what these positions make possible: two new abilities the model gains once language has become geometry. Once every piece has a place, the model can do two things: it can now measure relatedness, and it can trace relationships like directions on a map.

i · the measuring

A word’s coordinates, taken together, are called a vector. A vector is a list of numbers that marks a position. Every word has a vector, and the distance between any two words is a number the model can calculate. Because each word is a list of numbers, the model can compare two words the way you might compare two locations on a map: take their coordinates and calculate how far apart they sit. Nothing consults a dictionary; the distance comes straight from the numbers. A high level of similarity between two words means that these words appeared in similar sentences during the training process, and a low level of similarity means they did not. This is the first useful thing a position creates. The model never needs to be told that “beam” belongs with “light”, or that “water” does not belong with “song”. The training placed them at their distances, and now the LLM can simply measure the closeness, or proximity, for any pair of words. Real models make this measurement with a calculation called cosine similarity, which compares the directions of two vectors. ii · the turning

The second thing positions create is stranger. In this space, a relationship between two words becomes a direction: the step you would take to get from one to the other. Once the model finds a direction, it can reuse it somewhere else. Below, we can see that the step from seahorse to sea is the step from a creature to the place it lives.

the step from one word to another

The landing point is not exactly on a word: the arithmetic gives a position, and the LLM finds the nearest word to it. The nearest word to bird plus the creature-to-home step is sky. The nearest word to the same step from moth is night. The step from moth to flame is a real direction too, but a different one: attraction rather than home. Each relationship runs along its own direction, and the trick only works when you reuse the same one. Try the step from sea and it lands near nothing, because this direction only means something when it starts with a creature. It exists as a direction that repeats across the space, because the training text used these pairs of words in repeated ways. This trick became famous in an early system called word2vec, where researchers found that the step from man to woman, applied to king, landed near queen. The technique is called vector arithmetic: subtracting one vector from another to find a relationship and adding it somewhere else to reuse it.

iii · the inheriting

However, the same researchers found something else. In that early system, the step from he to she, when applied to doctor, landed near nurse; applied to computer programmer, it landed near homemaker.

It’s important to remember that these directions record how words were actually used in the writing that the model was learning from. That writing contains the patterns of the people who wrote it, including patterns that we may question. New models are now trained with more care, but the principle itself has not changed: the geometry of the space (relationships between words) is based on the record of usage, not of truth. Follow a direction, and you are following a path of written history.

So, a position is not just about storage of words or points. For the model, it is a place from which it measures distances and takes directions. At this stage, this is all preparation. The model has not yet interpreted your sentence as a sentence. The next page shows this act of “reading”, where the separate pieces of a sentence begin to notice each other.

When the poems reach from beam to glimmer to echo, they are moving along these directions. Nearness in the space is how their words found each other.

an honest note

The spaces on this page show three hand-placed dimensions of the hundreds or thousands real models use, and their slow turning is presentational; real models measure similarity across all their dimensions at once, usually with a calculation called cosine similarity, which compares directions rather than plain distances. The similarity numbers here are calculated from distance on this toy map. The word2vec findings, including king and queen, and doctor and nurse, are documented results from research on real systems in the 2010s, not inventions of this page; in real spaces the arithmetic works approximately, not perfectly. The step shown here lands cleanly because we drew the map so that it would.
